Ghost Rovers

Somewhere on the Moon, a car is still parked.

Since 1969, people have sent machines to the Moon, Mars and beyond. When their jobs ended, they stayed there. Some went quiet. A few are still working. Meet eight of them, and the science each one made possible.

The Moon: left behind on purpose

Every kilogram carried home costs fuel, so astronauts left things behind. Some of those things still work.

Apollo laser mirrors

Still working

“I have no battery and no moving parts. I just wait for light.”

Why was I sent?
Scientists wanted the exact distance to the Moon. Apollo 11, 14 and 15 each left a panel of mirrors on the surface.
How did I work?
Each mirror is a “corner cube”, shaped like the inside corner of a box, so it bounces light straight back the way it came. Observatories on Earth fire a laser at the panel and time the round trip.
What did I find out?
The Moon is drifting away from Earth by about 3.8 centimeters a year, and the data gave evidence that the Moon has a fluid core.
Where am I now?
Still on the Moon and still used by scientists, more than 50 years later.

Lunar Roving Vehicle

Silent

“I folded up to fit inside the lunar module. Then I drove astronauts across the Moon.”

Why was I sent?
To let astronauts travel much farther than they could walk. Three of us flew, on Apollo 15, 16 and 17, in 1971 and 1972.
How did I work?
Batteries powered four electric motors. My design speed was about 13 km/h, but Apollo 17 commander Gene Cernan reached about 18 km/h, the unofficial Moon speed record.
What did I find out?
I carried astronauts and rocks. On Apollo 17 I drove 35.9 km and helped the crew collect and bring home more Moon rocks.
Where am I now?
All three of us are still parked on the Moon, right where the astronauts left us.

Mars: explorers that outlived their plans

These machines weren’t left on purpose. They kept going until something, usually dust or lost power, stopped them.

Sojourner

Silent

“I weighed 10.6 kilograms and stood one foot tall. I was the first rover on Mars.”

Why was I sent?
To test whether a tiny rover could drive around another planet. I rode to Mars on the Pathfinder lander, which landed on July 4, 1997.
How did I work?
Solar panels powered me, and I sent my messages through the Pathfinder lander, so I could never talk to Earth by myself.
What did I find out?
I carried an instrument that measured what rocks and soil are made of, and I sent back 550 pictures. With the lander, I found clues that Mars was once warm and wet. I also showed engineers that a small rover can explore Mars.
Where am I now?
The Pathfinder lander sent its last data on September 27, 1997, and the mission ended. I’m still on Mars.

Opportunity

Silent

“My team planned for 90 days. I gave them about 14 years.”

Why was I sent?
To look for signs that Mars once had liquid water. I landed on January 24, 2004.
How did I work?
Solar panels charged my batteries. Wind sometimes cleaned the dust off them, which is a big reason I lasted so long. I drove 45.16 km, an off-Earth record that stood until October 2026, when Perseverance passed it.
What did I find out?
Rocks that formed in water, which helped show that parts of ancient Mars were wet.
Where am I now?
A planet-wide dust storm blocked my sunlight in 2018. My last contact with Earth was on June 10, 2018, and NASA ended my mission in February 2019.

InSight

Silent

“I never moved. I just listened to Mars shake.”

Why was I sent?
To study the inside of Mars, such as how thick its crust is and what its core is like. I landed on November 26, 2018.
How did I work?
A very sensitive seismometer sat on the ground and felt marsquakes, the way seismometers feel earthquakes on Earth.
What did I find out?
I detected 1,319 marsquakes. They showed that Mars has a liquid core and helped scientists measure its crust.
Where am I now?
Dust slowly covered my solar panels until I ran out of power. My last contact with Earth was on December 15, 2022. I’m still in Elysium Planitia.

Ingenuity

Silent

“Nobody had ever flown a helicopter on another planet. I did it 72 times.”

Why was I sent?
To test whether flying is possible on Mars. I was planned for no more than five short flights in 30 days.
How did I work?
Mars’s air is less than 1% as thick as Earth’s, so my two sets of blades spun in opposite directions, very fast, to grab enough air to lift me.
What did I find out?
Powered, controlled flight works on another world. I flew 72 times and covered 17 km, and later I scouted the ground ahead for the Perseverance rover.
Where am I now?
My first flight was April 19, 2021 and my last was January 18, 2024, when my rotor blades were damaged. NASA ended my mission a week later. I’m still on Mars.

Deep space: still traveling, still talking

These probes left the neighborhood of the planets. One is still talking to us.

Voyager 1

Still working

“I launched in 1977. I’m still sending you messages.”

Why was I sent?
To fly past Jupiter and Saturn and send back close-up pictures and measurements.
How did I work?
My radio sends faint signals home across billions of kilometers. Earth’s big dish antennas listen for them.
What did I find out?
Active volcanoes on Jupiter’s moon Io, a thin ring around Jupiter, and new moons and a new ring at Saturn. In 2012 I became the first human-made object to enter interstellar space, the space between the stars.
Where am I now?
On November 18, 2026, I reach one light-day from Earth, about 25.9 billion kilometers. I’m the first human-made object ever that far away, and a radio signal needs a full 24 hours to reach me.

Pioneer 10

Silent, still traveling

“I was the first to cross the asteroid belt. Nobody knew if I would make it.”

Why was I sent?
To fly through the asteroid belt, the ring of rocky leftovers between Mars and Jupiter, and visit Jupiter. I launched on March 2, 1972.
How did I work?
The Sun is too faint out there for solar panels, so I made electricity from the heat of radioactive material.
What did I find out?
I proved a spacecraft can cross the asteroid belt safely, and in December 1973 I sent back the first close-up pictures of Jupiter.
Where am I now?
I was planned for about 21 months and worked for more than 30 years. My last faint signal reached Earth on January 23, 2003, from about 12 billion kilometers away. I’m still traveling, but I’ve gone silent.

Silent doesn’t mean finished.

Mirrors from 1969 still help scientists today. Mars landers and rovers taught engineers what works on another planet, and Perseverance has now passed Opportunity’s driving record. NASA’s planned SkyFall mission will build on Ingenuity by sending a team of next-generation Mars helicopters. Every machine here made the next mission possible.
